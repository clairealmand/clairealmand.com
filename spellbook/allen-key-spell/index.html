The Allen Key Spell

A spellbook of the hidden forces that make people buy, build, and believe.

Has a junior analyst ever walked you through the glory of their beautiful, million-cell spreadsheet?

No summary, of course. You get to parse every tab yourself.

And they're beaming the whole time, because they built it.

Someone else casts a spell and you clap. Cast it yourself, and suddenly you're a wizard.

That glow is this week's spell: we love what we help build.

It's called the IKEA effect. It's why the wobbly bookshelf you built yourself feels like a legendary artifact, while the sturdy one from the store is just… furniture.

In this issue: the Allen Key Spell. Why we love what we help build, how to cast it at work, and where it backfires.

Welcome to Growth Grimoire, a spellbook of the hidden forces that make people buy, build, and believe. A grimoire is a book of spells. This one holds marketing's closest thing to real magic: the psychology of why people do what they do. Each issue translates one idea from academic into human, then turns it into something you can try at work, immediately.

✨ In a world getting crowded with AI, I don't want us to forget the main pillar of marketing: the customer. You know, us humans. ✨

📜 The Lore: why we overvalue what's ours

We value things more once they're ours, and even more when we make them. The recipe has two ingredients, plus a reason it works.

1. Owning it. In 1989, economist Jack Knetsch gave students either a coffee mug or a chocolate bar, then offered a straight swap.

  • Almost nobody traded: 89% kept the mug, and 90% kept the chocolate.
  • A third group that simply picked one split close to even.
  • A few minutes of ownership made each gift feel worth more. That's the endowment effect.

2. Making it. In 2012, Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon, and Dan Ariely had volunteers assemble IKEA storage boxes.

  • Builders bid about 63% more for their own boxes than others bid for identical pre-built ones.

3. Why? Researchers have two leading explanations, and they can work together.

  • Feeling capable. Making something proves we can, and a follow-up study by the same team found this sense of competence explains much of why we value our own work more. It's the glow behind every spreadsheet show-and-tell.
  • Justifying the effort. Most of us assume our beliefs drive our actions. Research shows it also runs the other way: our actions can change our beliefs. When the two clash, we feel uneasy. That's cognitive dissonance, and we often fix it by changing the belief: "I spent an hour on this shelf, so it must be worth it." In a 1959 study by Elliot Aronson and Judson Mills, students who passed an embarrassing test to join an exceedingly dull group liked it more.

✨ Practical Magic

The trick: let people build part of it themselves. Build-A-Bear has built a whole business on this. The store makes the bear. The kid picks it, helps stuff it, and kisses a little heart before it goes inside. (Yes, really. There's a ceremony.) The store did 90% of the work. The kid did the part that makes it theirs. It's the IKEA effect, now with stuffing.

In onboarding, ask for one real choice up front. Duolingo asks how many minutes a day you want to practice before you've learned a single word. Tiny question. But now the goal is yours, and the endowment effect kicks in. (The owl makes sure of it.) If you sell sales-reporting software, ask new users what they care about most right now (new leads, closed deals, or revenue), then build their first dashboard around the answer.

Never hand a new customer an empty account. A blank dashboard is homework. Instead, give them a template they can finish with their own details, like a starter budget with their real numbers or a first report with their logo on it. On day one, they have something useful, and they built it themselves. That's the "feeling capable" part of the Lore: they just proved they can use your product.

In planning meetings, leave a few decisions open. Build the plan and lock in the non-negotiables (you're the expert, in theory 🙃). Leave the other decisions for the team. It's faster than a blank slate, and people defend a plan they helped build. That's justifying the effort: they worked on it, so it must be good.

☠️ Curse Collection: the frictionless paradox

We're trained to cut steps. Every extra field is a chance to drop off, and the shortest signup almost always wins the A/B test.

But some steps aren't chores. They're the moment customers put something of themselves into the product.

Picture a budgeting app. Step three asks new users to pick one savings goal, like "Trip to Japan." The growth team cuts it, and signups go up. Three months later, fewer people are opening the app. They never made anything in it, so there's nothing to own. They aren't invested (pun intended!).

Your car insurer is probably counting on this too. In 2019, the UK's financial regulator found that 6 million home and car insurance customers had paid high prices the year before. Many were loyal customers who never switched. If they'd paid the average price for their risk, they'd have saved £1.2 billion. That's status quo bias, a close cousin of the endowment effect. Once something is ours, leaving feels like a loss, even when staying costs more.

Most tests only count signups, so nobody connects the cut step to the drop in retention.

The fine print: the effort has to end in success. In Norton's study, people who started building but didn't finish valued their box no more than anyone else did. No finished box, no IKEA effect.

We've all been there. The file upload fails twice, and the error message just says "Something went wrong." That's effort with no finished box, so nobody falls in love with the product. They close the tab, usually with a few choice words.

If the answer might be no, say it early. Imagine an application that asks for 30 documents, an arm, a leg, and the Goblet of Fire. Then it rejects you for something it could have checked on day one. By then you've put in hours, and your brain needs that effort to have been worth it (hello again, cognitive dissonance). So you blame whoever wasted your time.

The counter-spell: cut the chores, keep the choices.

  • Ask upfront for an action that's small, meaningful, and easy to finish.
  • If you might say no, say it before people get invested.
  • Before you cut an optional or high-effort step, look past signups. Does it help people get something useful out of the product and give them a reason to come back?

⚗️ The Trial: the wand shop

Your turn to cast.

You sell wands. Good ones: hand-carved, perfectly balanced, ready to cast the moment they leave your shop.

The problem is your customers. They buy a wand, cast one spark, and lose interest. Half of them are back within a month asking for a refund. "It just doesn't feel like mine."

Meanwhile, the witch down the lane charges twice as much, and her customers never return their wands. They name them. One even knitted his wand a sweater.

You can't cut prices, and you can't hire more wizards.
