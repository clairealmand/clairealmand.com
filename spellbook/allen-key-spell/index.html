✨ In a world getting crowded with AI, I don't want us to forget the main pillar of marketing: the customer. You know, us humans.

This week's spell: we love what we help build.

It's called the IKEA effect. It's why the wobbly bookshelf you built yourself feels like a legendary artifact, while the sturdy one from the store is furniture.

📜 The Lore: why we overvalue what's ours

Our brains add a markup when something is ours, and a bigger one when we made it. The recipe has three ingredients.

1. Owning it. In 1989, economist Jack Knetsch gave students either a coffee mug or a chocolate bar, then offered a straight swap.

  • Almost nobody traded: 89% kept the mug, and 90% kept the chocolate.
  • A third group that simply picked one split close to even.
  • A few minutes of ownership made each gift feel worth more. That's the endowment effect.

2. Making it. In 2012, Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon, and Dan Ariely had volunteers assemble IKEA storage boxes.

  • Builders bid about 63% more for their own boxes than others bid for identical pre-built ones.

3. Why it happens. Researchers have two leading explanations, and they can work together.

  • Feeling capable. Making something proves we can, and a follow-up study by the same team found this sense of competence explains much of the markup. It's the glow behind every spreadsheet show-and-tell.
  • Justifying the effort. Most of us assume our beliefs drive our actions. Research shows it also runs the other way: our actions can change our beliefs. When the two clash, we feel uneasy. That's cognitive dissonance, and we often fix it by changing the belief: "I spent an hour on this shelf, so it must be worth it." In a 1959 study by Elliot Aronson and Judson Mills, students who passed an embarrassing test to join a dull group liked it more.

✨ Practical Magic

The trick: hand people a small piece of the building. These studies ran in labs, so treat these as ideas to test.

  • Give onboarding a crafting moment. Before the dashboard appears, let new users name their workspace, pick the metric they care about, or set a first milestone.
  • Help customers make something useful on day one. A starter budget or first report gives them something that's theirs, plus the feeling of "I figured it out."
  • Build the straw person yourself. I bring a plan with its shape set (I'm the expert, in theory 🙃), then let the team knock it down and riff. It's faster than a blank slate, and people back what they helped shape.

☠️ Curse Collection: the frictionless paradox

Growth teams, this one's for you.

You're trained to cut steps. Every field and click is a chance to lose someone, so the shortest flow often wins the test.

But some steps are where customers make the product theirs. Cut those, and sign-ups can rise while the reasons to come back shrink. The test reads out this week. The cost shows up later in retention, where few teams look.

The fine print: effort has to succeed. In Norton's study, people who didn't finish their build lost the premium. A confusing setup step creates frustration with a progress bar, not ownership.

From the field: effort raises the stakes of a no. This one's from my years in lending, not a lab.

In-person events cost an eerie amount of time and money, and something always goes wrong. But in my experience, the people who show up are much more likely to apply. The effort it took to get there seems to be part of why. If they turn out not to be eligible, the no stings far more. Webinars work the same way.

That's the power of an early conditional offer: people know where they stand before the heavy lifting, and that earns goodwill.

The counter-spell: cut the chores, keep the choices.

  • Ask for effort that's small, meaningful, and easy to win.
  • If the answer might be no, say so before people do the work.
  • Measure who finishes and who comes back, not just who signs up.

⚗️ The Trial: put it all together

Your turn to cast. No trick options: all three moves are reasonable. Each gives users the same useful starter budget, with no bank connection needed, and naming the budget plus choosing one savings goal takes 60 seconds.

Your team's budgeting app takes four minutes to set up, and too many people quit halfway. The product lead wants it much shorter. You can run one test this quarter, and all three versions are equally easy to build. Which would you test first?

  • A. Setup first. Users name their budget and choose a savings goal before the budget appears.
  • B. Value first. The budget appears right away, followed by an optional prompt to name it and choose a goal.
  • C. Setup optional. The budget appears right away. Naming it and choosing a goal live in settings, with no prompt.

Tell me in the comments on LinkedIn: A, B or C, why, and what you'd measure. My pick opens the next issue.
